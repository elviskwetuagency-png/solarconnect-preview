Heating water is one of the biggest electricity costs in many Kenyan homes, guest houses and rentals. Electric showers and instant heaters typically draw around 3,000W or more each while running. A solar water heater takes most of that load off your bill by heating water directly from the sun, without needing an inverter or batteries.

Why solar water heating pays off

  • It uses heat directly. A solar water heater captures the sun's heat, which is far more efficient than turning sunlight into electricity and then back into heat.
  • It protects your solar power system. Running electric showers from a battery system drains it quickly. Moving water heating to a solar water heater lets a smaller PV system cover the rest of the house (see our guide to sizing a solar system).
  • Hot water is stored. An insulated tank keeps water hot into the evening and next morning.

Heat-pipe vs flat-plate collectors

Pressurized heat-pipe (evacuated tube)

Each glass tube has a vacuum layer that insulates it, with a copper heat pipe inside that carries heat up to a manifold where it warms the water. Because the vacuum cuts heat loss, heat-pipe systems keep performing well in cooler, windy or partly cloudy weather. The water does not flow through the tubes themselves, which also makes them a good option where water is hard and causes scaling.

Direct flat-plate

A flat-plate collector is an insulated panel with a dark absorber plate and pipes behind glass. In a direct system the water passes through the collector itself. Flat-plate collectors are robust, have no glass tubes to break and suit sunny sites with good roof space. In very hard water areas, scaling inside a direct collector is something to plan for.

Pressurized vs low-pressure systems

All the solar water heaters we currently stock are pressurized systems. A pressurized system delivers hot water at close to mains pressure, so showers and mixer taps work as they would with an electric heater. It needs a tank rated for pressure and safety valves, which come as part of a proper installation.

Low-pressure (gravity) systems are cheaper but rely on the tank's height for pressure, which often gives a weak shower flow.

Installation tips

  • Avoid shade. Mount the collector where it gets sun for most of the day, clear of water tanks, trees and taller buildings.
  • Keep a slight tilt. Kenya is close to the equator, so the sun is high for much of the year. A modest tilt lets rain wash dust off the collector.
  • Check the roof. A full 300L tank of water is heavy, so the roof structure must be able to carry it.
  • Plan for cloudy spells. Many installations include an electric backup element for long cloudy periods. Ask us whether the model you choose includes one.
  • Keep pipe runs short and insulated so hot water reaches the tap quickly and stays hot.

FAQs

Does a solar water heater work on cloudy days?

Yes, at reduced output. Heat-pipe collectors cope best with cloud and cool weather, and the insulated tank stores heat from sunnier hours. For long cloudy spells, an electric backup element keeps water hot.

Is a 200 litre solar water heater enough for a family of four?

For most families of four, yes. At 40–50 litres per person per day, four people need about 160–200 litres. If you take long showers or run a washing machine on hot water, consider 300L.

Do I need batteries or an inverter for a solar water heater?

No. A solar water heater heats water directly from the sun and stores it in an insulated tank, so it does not need batteries, panels or an inverter.

Heat-pipe or flat-plate for hard borehole water?

Heat-pipe systems are generally better with hard water because the water stays in the tank rather than flowing through the collector tubes, which reduces scaling in the collector.
